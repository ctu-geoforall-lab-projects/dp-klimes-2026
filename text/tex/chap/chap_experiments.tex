\chapter{Experimental Results}
\label{chap:experiments}

This chapter presents the results of all experiments defined in Section~\ref{sec:transfer}. The chapter is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:s2_training} reports the results of Sentinel-2 model training, which provides the pretrained model used as the starting point for all transfer learning experiments. Section~\ref{sec:baseline_results} presents the baseline results, including the from-scratch VEN$\mu$S model and MAJA. Sections~\ref{sec:transductive_results} and~\ref{sec:inductive_results} report the results of the transductive and inductive transfer learning experiments. Finally, Section~\ref{sec:comparative_analysis} summarizes the results across all experiments and addresses the research questions defined in Section~\ref{sec:transfer}. 

For each experiment, per-class and overall metrics for all normalization strategies are reported in the main text, while confusion matrices and visual examples of predicted cloud masks are shown for the best performing normalization strategy only. Confusion matrices for all normalization variants and additional visual prediction examples for the best performing normalization strategy are provided in Appendix~\ref{chap:appendix}.

\section{Sentinel-2 Model Training}
\label{sec:s2_training}

Before transfer learning experiments can be conducted, a U-Net model is trained on the Sentinel-2 dataset for each applicable normalization strategy. Fixed Z-score with VEN$\mu$S statistics is not applicable here, as the model is trained entirely on Sentinel-2 data and VEN$\mu$S statistics have no meaningful interpretation in this context. The resulting three models serve as the initialization point for all subsequent transfer learning experiments. The S2 test set metrics are reported here as a reference for source domain performance.

The dynamic Z-score normalization produced substantially better results than both raw and fixed Z-score strategies across all metrics and all classes. The dynamic model achieved an mF1 of 0.865 and mIoU of 0.766, compared to 0.766 and 0.623 for fixed Z-score and 0.690 and 0.537 for raw data. This improvement can likely be explained by the per-tile normalization, which reduces radiometric differences between patches captured under varying atmospheric conditions and at different geographic locations, making the problem more consistent across the entire dataset.

Shadow detection shows the largest performance gap between normalization strategies. Dynamic Z-score achieves an F1 of 0.791 for shadow, compared to 0.706 for fixed Z-score and 0.534 for raw data. Shadow is consistently the hardest class across all normalizations. In the raw strategy, shadow recall is relatively high at 0.744 while precision is substantially lower at 0.417, suggesting that the model detects shadow regions, but produces predictions that extend beyond the annotated boundaries. This behaviour is likely caused by the spectral similarity of cloud shadows to other dark surfaces, compounded by the fact that shadow annotations were the most difficult to label accurately. This gap narrows with normalization, with dynamic Z-score achieving a more balanced precision of 0.818 and recall of 0.766.

The from-scratch model achieves strong performance across all normalization strategies, with mF1 values ranging from 0.938 to 0.954. Unlike the S2 model results, the raw strategy achieves the best overall performance, followed closely by fixed Z-score with VEN$\mu$S statistics and dynamic Z-score. This behaviour is likely caused by the geographic concentration of the VEN$\mu$S dataset. As all scenes cover a small, spectrally consistent region of Israel, the radiometric properties are similar across tiles. In this setting, per-tile normalization likely removes useful spectral information rather than harmful radiometric variation, which explains why dynamic Z-score underperforms relative to raw data. Despite achieving the lowest overall metrics, the dynamic Z-score model converges significantly faster at epoch 283, compared to 538 for raw and 596 for fixed Z-score. The differences between normalization strategies are small overall, with a gap of only 1.6 percentage points in mF1 between raw and dynamic, suggesting that for a geographically concentrated dataset with consistent radiometric properties, normalization strategy has less impact than for a dataset covering more diverse geographic regions and surface types.

The cloud-free class consistently outperforms the cloud class across all normalizations, which is consistent with findings reported in the cloud detection literature \cite{pesek2022, wright2025}.

\section{Transductive Transfer Learning}
\label{sec:transductive_results}

This section presents the results of the transductive transfer learning experiment (E0), in which the Sentinel-2 pretrained model is applied directly to VEN$\mu$S data without any modification to the model weights, addressing the first research question defined in Section~\ref{sec:transfer}. Each normalization variant uses the Sentinel-2 pretrained model trained with the corresponding normalization strategy. An exception is the fixed Z-score with VEN$\mu$S statistics variant, for which no corresponding Sentinel-2 pretrained model exists. In this case, the model pretrained with fixed Z-score with Sentinel-2 statistics is used, as it represents the closest available approximation in terms of normalization approach.

The results reveal a significant dependence on normalization strategy, with performance ranging from near-complete failure to strong cross-sensor generalization. The raw and fixed Z-score with Sentinel-2 statistics strategies both fail entirely at cloud detection, achieving cloud F1 scores of 0.005 and 0.000. Despite this, both strategies achieve relatively high overall accuracy of 0.767 and 0.798, which is a direct consequence of the class imbalance in the test set, where cloud-free pixels dominate. The model under these strategies predicts everything as cloud-free, which is reflected in the cloud-free recall values of 0.960 and 1.000. This illustrates why OA alone is a misleading metric for imbalanced datasets and highlights the importance of reporting other types of metrics.

The failure of the raw strategy confirms that spectral band alignment alone is insufficient for cross-sensor transfer when the radiometric distributions of the two sensors differ. A key source of this difference is that the Sentinel-2 training data consists of Level~1C top-of-atmosphere reflectances, while VEN$\mu$S Level~2 products provide atmospherically corrected surface reflectances, resulting in systematically different value ranges across sensors. The failure of the fixed Z-score with Sentinel-2 statistics strategy is consistent with this difference, as VEN$\mu$S values fall far outside the normalized range defined by the Sentinel-2 training distribution.

Fixed Z-score normalization with VEN$\mu$S statistics achieves a substantially better cloud F1 of 0.659 and mF1 of 0.758, demonstrating that normalizing the target data closer to its own distribution enables meaningful cloud detection. However, the results reveal a precision-recall imbalance for the cloud class, with a recall of 0.965 but precision of only 0.500, which indicates that the model detects most cloud pixels but produces a large number of false positives. The corresponding cloud-free recall drops to 0.756, suggesting significant overdetection of clouds. This behaviour is consistent with the residual domain shift between the sensors. While Z-score normalization aligns the mean and standard deviation of the distributions, it does not account for differences in histogram shape, which may arise from the distinct spectral response functions of the two sensors and atmospheric correction procedures of the two datasets. As a consequence, normalized VEN$\mu$S values fall in regions of the feature space that the model associates with clouds. It should additionally be noted that this variant uses the model pretrained with fixed Z-score with Sentinel-2 statistics, as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:transfer}. This introduces an additional inconsistency between training and inference normalization that likely contributes to the observed overdetection.

Dynamic Z-score normalization achieves by far the strongest performance, with an mF1 of 0.919 and mIoU of 0.853. The cloud class achieves an F1 of 0.874 with a well-balanced precision of 0.793 and recall of 0.973, while the cloud-free class achieves an F1 of 0.963. This result demonstrates that per-tile normalization, by standardizing each tile independently from its own pixel statistics, effectively compensates for the radiometric gap between sensors regardless of the absolute value ranges or distribution shapes involved. Notably, the dynamic Z-score result of mF1 0.919 approaches the from-scratch baseline of 0.954, suggesting that in the absence of labeled target domain data, a well-chosen normalization strategy alone can achieve strong cross-sensor transfer performance.

Comparing E1 against the transductive setting, output layer fine-tuning substantially improves performance under raw and fixed S2 normalization, demonstrating that even minimal target domain adaptation has a large impact when the normalization strategy is poorly suited for cross-sensor transfer. Under dynamic Z-score, the improvement is smaller, from 0.919 to 0.946, consistent with the fact that dynamic Z-score already achieved strong performance in the transductive setting.

Addressing the first research question, the transductive experiment demonstrates that spectral band alignment alone is insufficient for effective cross-sensor transfer, with raw normalization achieving an mF1 of only 0.436. The large radiometric gap between the two sensors is largely attributable to the difference in product level. The Sentinel-2 training data consists of Level~1C top-of-atmosphere reflectances, while VEN$\mu$S Level~2 products provide atmospherically corrected surface reflectances, resulting in substantially different value ranges. However, with dynamic Z-score normalization, the transductive model achieves an mF1 of 0.919, demonstrating that input-level radiometric normalization has a decisive impact on zero-shot cross-sensor generalization. This result shows that a well-chosen normalization strategy can largely bridge the radiometric gap between sensors without any modification to the model weights.

Regarding the second research question, frozen encoder fine-tuning achieves the strongest performance among all inductive strategies, reaching an mF1 of 0.961 and surpassing both output-layer fine-tuning (0.952) and full fine-tuning (0.956). The best performing strategy demonstrates that fine-tuning the decoder together with the output layer provides the optimal degree of network adaptation for cross-sensor transfer between these two sensors. However, even output layer fine-tuning alone achieves an mF1 of 0.952, which is just below the from-scratch baseline of 0.954. The result suggests that even minimal adaptation is sufficient for effective cross-sensor transfer between these two sensors. Full fine-tuning of the entire network does not provide additional benefit and slightly reduces performance compared to frozen encoder fine-tuning, though the difference is small at 0.005 mF1.

Addressing the third research question, all inductive experiments achieve performance comparable to the from-scratch baseline of mF1 0.954. Even the weakest inductive result, achieved by output layer fine-tuning without normalization, reaches an mF1 of 0.923, only 3.1 percentage points below the baseline. The best result, achieved by frozen encoder fine-tuning with fixed Z-score normalization using VEN$\mu$S statistics, reaches an mF1 of 0.961, surpassing the from-scratch baseline by 0.007 mF1 points. This demonstrates that transfer learning from Sentinel-2 can match, and in some cases even slightly exceed, the performance of a model trained entirely on VEN$\mu$S data, reducing the need for large amounts of manually labeled target domain data. MAJA, evaluated visually only due to its known limitation of overestimating cloud cover, clearly underperforms all deep learning approaches.
